# Article

Relocating can make an ordinary home sale feel much more complicated.

Maybe you accepted a new job. Maybe you are moving closer to family. Maybe you are leaving Ohio for a new opportunity, or moving within the state on a schedule you did not choose.

Either way, you now have two major projects happening at once:

**Moving your life**

and

**Deciding what to do with your current house.**

You do not need a perfect answer on day one.

But you do need a clear plan before the move turns your house into a long-distance responsibility.

## Start With Your Actual Timeline

The first question is not:

**“How do I get the highest possible price for my house?”**

It is:

**“When do I realistically need to be done dealing with this house?”**

Your answer may depend on:

* Your start date at a new job
* A lease or closing date in your new location
* School schedules
* Family obligations
* Whether you have already purchased another home
* Whether you can afford to carry two properties at once
* Whether you need the equity from this house for your next move

Those details affect which selling path makes sense.

A homeowner who has six months and can manage showings may make a different decision from someone moving in three weeks who needs certainty about when the house will be sold.

There is nothing wrong with wanting the highest possible sale price.

But it is only one part of the outcome.

## Decide Whether You Want to Sell Before or After You Move

There is no single right answer.

### Selling Before You Relocate

Selling before the move can make life simpler.

You may be able to finish one chapter before starting the next. You avoid maintaining a vacant house from another state, and you do not have to coordinate repairs, showings, or inspections remotely.

But selling before you move may also mean living through the sale process.

That can involve preparing the house, keeping it presentable, scheduling showings, accommodating photographers and inspectors, and responding to buyer requests while you are also packing and making plans for your move.

For some people, that is manageable.

For others, it is too much to take on at the same time.

### Selling After You Relocate

Selling after you move may give you more flexibility with packing and moving out.

But the house may then become vacant, bringing its own set of [costs, upkeep, and decisions](/vacant-property).

That means you may need to continue paying for insurance, taxes, utilities, maintenance, and possibly the mortgage while also paying for housing in your new location.

You may also need someone local to check on the property, provide access, handle repairs, mow the lawn, or respond if there is a problem.

A house can be easier to show once it is empty.

But empty does not always mean simple.

The useful question is:

**Which option creates the least amount of risk and disruption for my actual move?**

## Understand the Cost of Carrying Two Homes

If you are relocating before your current house sells, you may be carrying more than you expect.

Possible costs can include:

* Mortgage payments
* Property taxes
* Insurance
* Utilities
* Lawn care or snow removal
* Repairs and maintenance
* HOA costs, if applicable
* Travel back to the property
* The cost of your new home or rental

You do not need a complicated financial model.

Start with this:

**How much will it cost me each month if this house is still mine after I move?**

Then multiply that by a realistic number of months.

The answer does not automatically mean you need to sell quickly.

But it helps you understand how much flexibility you actually have.

## Selling Before You Move Does Not Always Mean Listing Immediately

If you have time, a traditional listing may make sense.

Listing can expose your home to more buyers and may produce a higher sale price. But it also comes with a process.

Depending on the house, that may include:

* Repairs or improvements
* Cleaning and staging
* Photography
* Showings
* Open houses
* Inspections
* Buyer repair requests
* Appraisal and financing timelines
* Negotiations and possible delays before closing

That process may be worth it if maximizing market exposure is your priority and your move timeline allows for it.

But relocation often changes the value of convenience and certainty.

If you are trying to start a new job, settle your family, arrange a move, or close on another home, an extra few months of work around your old house may have a real cost—even if it does not appear clearly on a spreadsheet.

## What If the House Needs Work?

A move can make ordinary repairs feel much bigger.

Maybe the house needs cleaning, paint, flooring, an HVAC repair, roof work, or a longer list of [major repairs](/major-repairs).

If you were staying in the area, you might be willing to get estimates, choose contractors, oversee the work, and wait for the project to finish.

Once you are relocating, that may no longer feel practical.

Before starting work, ask:

**Will making these repairs leave me meaningfully better off after the cost, time, and added carrying expenses?**

For example:

Expected sale price after repairs
− Repair costs
− Carrying costs during the work and sale process
− Selling costs
= **Estimated net proceeds**

Then compare that with selling the property in its current condition.

The difference may justify the work.

Or it may be smaller than you expected.

## Should You Rent the House Instead?

Relocation sometimes makes people consider turning their home into a rental.

That can be a good decision in the right situation.

But do not make it only because selling feels inconvenient right now.

A rental is not simply a house you did not sell.

It is an investment and a business responsibility.

Think about:

* Expected rent
* Mortgage, taxes, and insurance
* Repairs and maintenance
* Vacancy between tenants
* Property-management costs
* Whether you want to manage the property from another location
* Whether you have enough reserves for major repairs
* Whether the rent actually justifies the responsibility

A good question is:

**If I did not already own this house, would I choose to buy it today as a rental investment?**

If the answer is yes, keeping it may make sense.

If the answer is no, selling may be the cleaner decision.

## You Do Not Need to Be in Ohio to Sell Your House

Moving does not automatically mean you have to return for every part of the sale.

But the process matters.

With a traditional listing, you may still need to coordinate access, respond to inspection issues, review offers, and make decisions from a distance.

With any sale, make sure you understand the closing process, what documents need to be signed, and whether anything requires your in-person involvement. Your title company, attorney, agent, or other closing professional can explain the requirements for your transaction.

The important point is this:

**Do not assume that leaving the state means the house will take care of itself.**

Make a plan for the property before you go.

## When a Direct Sale May Make Sense

A direct as-is sale may be a good option when speed, simplicity, and a coordinated closing date matter more than preparing the house for the full retail market.

Depending on the property and terms, it may allow you to:

* Sell without making repairs first
* Avoid preparing for repeated showings
* Avoid open houses
* Avoid coordinating contractors before the sale
* Sell before or after your move on a timeline that works for you
* Reduce the chance that your move is delayed by a buyer's mortgage approval
* Avoid agent commissions when no brokerage agreement requires payment

There is a trade-off.

**A direct offer may be lower than the price you could potentially achieve through full market exposure.**

That is why the right comparison is not simply:

**“What could the house list for?”**

It is:

**“What would I realistically keep after the time, cost, uncertainty, and work required to sell it that way?”**

## The Best Move Plan Is the One You Can Actually Carry Out

Relocating already involves a lot of decisions.

Your home sale should support the move—not become the reason it gets delayed, more expensive, or more stressful.

Start with your real deadline.

Understand what it will cost to keep the house after you leave.

Then compare the options based on the outcome that matters to you: price, timing, certainty, convenience, or the ability to move on without managing a house from far away.

For some homeowners, preparing and listing is the right choice.

For others, renting makes sense.

And for some, selling as-is on a clear timeline is the best way to close one chapter and focus on the next.

## Questions and Answers

### Should I Sell My House Before or After I Relocate?

Either option can make sense. Selling before you move can keep you from managing a vacant property from a distance, but it may mean handling showings, inspections, and packing at the same time. Selling after you move can make the move itself easier, but you may still be responsible for the mortgage, taxes, insurance, utilities, upkeep, and any problems that arise at the empty house. Start with your actual deadline and the amount of responsibility you are willing to carry after the move.

### Can I Sell My House After Moving Out of State?

Yes. Moving out of Ohio does not automatically prevent you from selling the house. You may still need to coordinate access, review offers, respond to inspection issues, and understand how closing documents will be handled. Before you leave, make a plan for the property and confirm the closing requirements for your particular transaction.

### What If I Cannot Afford to Carry Two Homes?

First, calculate what your current house will cost each month after you move, including the mortgage, taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance, and any travel or management costs. Then compare that total with your expected sale timeline and the equity you need for your next home. Knowing the real monthly cost can help you decide how much flexibility you have.

### Should I Rent My House Instead of Selling It?

Renting can make sense when the expected rent justifies the expenses and responsibility of owning a rental property. But it should be a deliberate investment decision, not simply a way to avoid selling during a busy move. Consider repairs, vacancy between tenants, property-management costs, reserves for major expenses, and whether you want to manage the property from another location.

### Can I Sell As-Is While I Am Relocating?

Yes. A direct as-is sale can let you consider selling without making repairs, staging the house, hosting repeated showings, or delaying your move.
