# Article

There are plenty of reasons a landlord may decide it's time to sell.

Maybe managing tenants is taking more time than it used to. Maybe the property needs another round of repairs. Maybe you're managing it from out of state. Maybe the return simply isn't as attractive as it once was.

Or maybe nothing is particularly wrong.

You may simply have a better use for the equity.

Selling a rental property doesn't mean the investment failed. Sometimes selling is simply the next business decision.

So before worrying about how to sell, start with a simpler question:

**Does keeping this property still make sense for you?**

## Should You Keep the Rental or Sell It?

Don't look only at the rent check.

Look at what the property is actually producing after everything required to own it.

**Start with:**

**Monthly rent**

− Mortgage

− Property taxes and insurance

− Maintenance and repairs

− Property management, if applicable

− Vacancy and other recurring property expenses

= **Estimated monthly cash flow**

This doesn't need to be exact. The goal is simply to get an honest picture of what the property is actually putting in your pocket each month.

Then look ahead. If the roof, HVAC, plumbing, or another major expense is coming, factor that into your decision too.

Then consider two things that don't appear as neatly on a spreadsheet:

**Your time and your equity.**

A property producing a few hundred dollars per month may look attractive until you consider the time it requires and how much of your equity is tied up in it.

One useful question is:

**If you didn't already own this property, would you buy it today at its current value?**

If the answer is yes, keeping it may still make sense.

If the answer is no—or you're simply ready to put the equity somewhere else—it may be time to compare your exit options.

## If You've Decided to Sell, You Have Two Main Paths

For most landlords, the decision comes down to:

**Sell on the open market**

or

**Sell directly off-market.**

Both can get the property sold.

But they are very different processes.

### Selling On-Market

Listing the property can give you exposure to a larger pool of buyers and may produce a higher sale price.

That can make sense when maximizing market exposure is your priority and you're willing to go through the process required to get it.

Depending on the property and tenant situation, that may include:

- Preparing the property for market.
- Making repairs or improvements.
- Coordinating access with tenants.
- Scheduling photographs and showings.
- Keeping the property presentable.
- Working through inspections.
- Negotiating repair requests or concessions.
- Waiting for buyer financing.
- Paying applicable agent commissions and other selling costs.

None of that means listing is a bad option.

For some landlords, the extra work is worth it. For others, a simpler sale may be a better fit.

### Selling Off-Market

If you've already decided that you want to sell the rental, an off-market sale can be a much simpler way to exit.

Instead of preparing the property for the retail market, you sell directly to a buyer who is evaluating the property as an investment.

That can eliminate many of the moving parts landlords would otherwise have to manage.

Depending on the buyer and agreed terms, an off-market sale may allow you to:

- Sell the property as-is.
- Avoid making repairs before selling.
- Sell with tenants still in place.
- Avoid open houses.
- Avoid repeated showings.
- Reduce disruption to the tenants.
- Avoid agent commissions when no brokerage agreement requires payment.
- Choose a closing timeline that works for you.
- Reduce dependence on traditional buyer financing.
- Deal directly with the person or company buying the property.

For a landlord who is already ready to move on, that simplicity has real value.

But there is an obvious trade-off:

**An off-market offer may be lower than the price you could potentially achieve by exposing the property to the full market.**

That's why the right comparison isn't simply:

**MLS price vs. off-market offer.**

## Don't Compare Sale Prices. Compare What You Actually Keep.

Suppose you believe the property could sell for $150,000 on the open market and you receive a $140,000 off-market offer.

At first glance, the difference looks like $10,000.

But that's not necessarily the real difference.

The better comparison is:

### On-Market

Expected sale price − repairs − commissions − selling/carrying costs = **Estimated net proceeds**

### Off-Market

Off-market offer − seller costs = **Estimated net proceeds**

Now compare those two numbers.

Maybe listing still leaves substantially more money in your pocket.

If so, the additional work may be worth it.

But sometimes the difference becomes much smaller once you stop comparing headline prices and start comparing net proceeds.

Then there is another part of the decision that doesn't fit neatly into the equation:

**Time.**

**Certainty.**

**Tenant disruption.**

**Your involvement.**

Only you can decide what those are worth.

## What About the Tenants?

You don't necessarily need to remove good tenants just because you want to sell the property.

The importance of the tenant situation depends heavily on how you decide to sell.

On the open market, an occupied property can affect access, showings, timing, and the types of buyers interested in the house.

A buyer who wants to occupy the property personally may view an existing tenancy very differently from an investor who is comfortable purchasing a tenant-occupied rental.

In an off-market investor sale, the tenant may be much less of an obstacle.

At Your House in Cash, we're comfortable evaluating occupied rental properties. You don't need to make the property vacant simply so we can consider buying it.

The specific lease and tenant rights still matter to the transaction, but having tenants in the property does not automatically prevent an off-market sale.

The easiest way is to get a real offer.

Not an online estimate.

Not a hypothetical number.

A price that an actual buyer is prepared to pay for the property in its current condition.

It answers a more practical question:

**What is one serious off-market buyer actually willing to pay for this rental as it sits today?**

Once you have that number, you can compare it with what you believe you'd actually net from listing.

## Make Sure You're Comparing a Real Buyer, Not Just a Big Number

Price matters.

But an offer only has value if the buyer actually closes.

Landlords selling off-market should look beyond the number and understand who they're dealing with.

Ask:

- Is the buyer actually purchasing the property?
- Is the offer dependent on financing?
- Are there inspection or cancellation provisions that could change the deal later?
- Are the fees clearly disclosed?
- Is the buyer comfortable with the existing tenant situation?
- Does the proposed closing timeline actually work for you?
- What happens if the property needs more work than expected?

A slightly higher offer isn't necessarily better if the buyer renegotiates late in the process or can't close.

**Price and certainty should be evaluated together.**

## Selling Doesn't Have to Become Another Property-Management Project

If your rental still makes sense to own, keep it.

If the numbers work and you still want to be a landlord, there is no reason to sell simply because selling is an option.

But if you've already decided that you'd rather have the equity than the property, you don't necessarily need to spend the next several months preparing the house, coordinating with tenants, making repairs, scheduling showings, and waiting for a traditional buyer.

You can test the off-market option first.

**Send us the property details and we'll give you a no-obligation as-is cash offer.**

Then compare it with what you realistically expect to keep from an on-market sale.

If listing makes more sense, you don't have to accept our offer.

If the numbers are close enough that avoiding the repairs, showings, tenant coordination, commissions, uncertainty, and extra work is worth it to you, an off-market sale may be the better exit.

Either way, you'll have a real number to make the decision with.
