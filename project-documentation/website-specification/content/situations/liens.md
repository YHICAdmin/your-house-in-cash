# Article

Finding out that there is a lien against your house can be unsettling.

Maybe it appeared while you were getting ready to sell. Maybe it is connected to unpaid property taxes, an old contractor bill, a judgment, a loan, or an obligation you thought had already been handled.

The important thing is not to jump immediately to:

**“I cannot sell this house.”**

Many houses with liens are sold.

But a lien changes the financial picture and adds coordination that needs to happen before closing.

Start with a simpler question:

**What is recorded against this property, how much is required to resolve it, and what would be left after the sale?**

## First, Separate the House From the Debt

The word “lien” is often used as if it means every kind of debt.

It does not.

A lien is a legal claim that can affect property. Some liens are connected directly to the house, such as a mortgage. Others can arise from taxes, construction work, court judgments, or other obligations.

The details matter.

For example, an Ohio judgment can become a lien on a debtor’s land in a county when the required certificate of judgment is filed. ([Ohio Revised Code][1]) Delinquent real-estate taxes can also be enforced through a lien and, if left unresolved, can lead to a tax-foreclosure action. ([Ohio Revised Code][2])

That does not tell you the answer for your house.

It tells you why the first step is to identify the actual recorded issue rather than relying on a vague memory of an old bill or a letter you received.

## A Lien Can Affect the Sale Without Preventing a Sale

A lien can make it harder to deliver the type of title a buyer expects at closing.

That is different from saying a voluntary sale is impossible.

In many transactions, the sale proceeds are used to pay the mortgage, lien, taxes, or other obligations that must be addressed for closing. The closing process then coordinates the documentation needed to show how those items are being resolved.

The practical question is:

**Will the sale produce enough money to cover the amounts that need to be paid and the costs of selling?**

If the answer is yes, the lien may be a closing issue to coordinate rather than a reason the house cannot be sold.

If the answer is no, the situation is more complicated. A buyer’s offer alone does not automatically make an unpaid balance disappear.

## Find the Real Payoff Amount

The amount you remember owing is not always the amount needed to close.

For a mortgage, interest and other charges can change the payoff. For a judgment or tax obligation, the amount due may include interest, penalties, costs, or other amounts that need to be verified.

That is why a useful financial picture starts with current numbers, not estimates from years ago.

You are trying to establish:

* The type of claim or lien
* Who holds it
* Whether it is recorded against the property
* The current amount needed to resolve it
* Whether there are deadlines, pending proceedings, or other requirements that affect timing

You do not need every answer at the first moment you learn about a lien.

But you do need to move from:

> “I think there is something against the house.”

toward:

> “These are the items we have identified, and this is what each one appears to require.”

## Do Not Confuse Sale Price With What You Keep

This is the key financial distinction.

Suppose you receive an offer of $180,000.

That does not mean you will receive $180,000 at closing.

A more useful starting point is:

**Sale price**

minus

**Mortgage payoff and lien payoffs**

minus

**Property taxes, closing costs, and other agreed selling costs**

equals

**Estimated proceeds to you**

If the house has several liens, the order in which claims are paid and the exact payoff requirements can matter. Do not assume that a large sale price automatically leaves enough for every obligation and money for you.

On the other hand, do not assume that a lien means there is no equity.

The house may still have meaningful value after the amounts required at closing are paid. You need the actual numbers before you can know.

## Why Timing Matters

A lien issue is easier to work through when there is time to gather information and coordinate the closing.

It becomes more difficult when a tax proceeding, foreclosure, court deadline, or another sale-related deadline is already approaching.

Ohio law provides ways for tax liens to be enforced through a sale proceeding. ([Ohio Revised Code][2]) That is one reason not to treat notices or recorded claims as something to sort out after the house is already under contract.

The goal is not to create urgency where none exists.

It is to understand whether the lien is simply an item to pay from closing, an issue that needs additional approval or documentation, or a problem that may prevent the transaction from closing on the proposed terms.

## There Are Usually Two Questions, Not One

When a house has a lien, people often ask only:

**“Can I sell it?”**

There is usually a second question:

**“Which selling process gives me the clearest path to a closing that actually works?”**

A traditional listing may make sense if you have time, the house is marketable, and broad exposure is the priority. It may also involve preparation, showings, inspections, buyer financing, and negotiations.

A direct as-is sale may make sense if the house needs work, the lien situation makes timing important, or you do not want to add repairs and repeated showings to an already complicated closing.

Neither route changes what must be paid to resolve the lien.

The difference is in the process, the expected price, the time involved, and the amount of work you want to take on.

## A Lien Is a Fact to Understand, Not a Conclusion About Your House

Having a lien can feel like the end of your options.

Usually, it is the beginning of a more specific set of questions.

What is the claim?

What is the current payoff?

What has to happen for closing?

And after those obligations are handled, what would actually remain?

Once you have those answers, you can evaluate the house based on the real outcome—not just the presence of a lien.

## Questions and Answers

### Can I Sell a House That Has a Lien?

Often, yes. A lien does not automatically make a voluntary sale impossible. It can affect whether the transaction can close and how the sale money is distributed. The key questions are what must be paid or otherwise resolved and whether the sale proceeds are enough to do that.

### Will the Lien Be Paid at Closing?

It may be paid from the sale proceeds if the transaction has enough money and the closing requirements are met. The exact process depends on the type of lien, its payoff requirements, and the terms of the sale. A lien is not automatically removed simply because the house is sold.

### What If I Owe More Than the House Will Sell For?

The sale may not produce enough to pay every mortgage, lien, tax obligation, and selling cost. In that situation, additional arrangements or approvals may be required before closing. Do not assume an offer for less than the total amount owed erases the difference.

### Are Unpaid Property Taxes a Lien on the House?

Unpaid real-estate taxes can lead to enforcement of a tax lien against the property under Ohio law. ([Ohio Revised Code][2]) Find out the current amount due and whether any tax-enforcement proceeding has begun before you decide how to sell.

### Does an As-Is Sale Remove the Lien Problem?

No. Selling as-is means the buyer evaluates the property in its current condition. It does not by itself eliminate recorded claims, payoff requirements, or the effect those obligations have on your proceeds. An as-is offer can still be useful because it gives you a sale number to compare with the amounts that must be paid.
