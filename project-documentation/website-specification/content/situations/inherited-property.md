If a family member recently died and left behind a house, you probably don’t need a complete lesson on probate law.

You need to know what matters now, who can make decisions about the house, whether it can be sold, and what you should figure out next.

Start there.

# First Steps After Inheriting a House

You do not need to decide today whether to keep, rent, renovate, or sell the house.

For now, make sure nothing preventable happens to it.

If the house is vacant, that generally means checking that it is secure, identifying urgent maintenance problems, and figuring out what is happening with insurance and essential services. A leaking pipe matters now. Whether the kitchen needs remodeling does not.

Also locate whatever estate and property documents are readily available—especially a will, trust, deed, or transfer-on-death paperwork.

You’re trying to answer one question:

**Who has the legal authority to deal with this house?**

That question determines much of what comes next.

# Probate and Who Can Act

Probate is the court-supervised process used to administer certain property after someone dies.

For the house, the important part is simpler:

**Probate may determine who has authority to deal with the property and what needs to happen before it can be sold.**

Not every inherited house has to pass through probate. How the house was owned matters. A trust, survivorship ownership, or a valid transfer-on-death designation can change how the property passes after death. If someone dies without a will, Ohio law determines who inherits based on the surviving family relationships. ([Ohio Revised Code][1]; [Ohio State Bar Association][4])

So don’t assume either of these:

> “The will says I inherit the house, so I can sell it.”

or:

> “Someone died, so the house must go through probate.”

The useful question is:

**How does this particular house pass, and who has authority to act?**

## Executor, administrator, heir—what’s the difference?

These terms sound more complicated than the underlying idea.

An **heir or beneficiary** is someone entitled to inherit.

An **executor or administrator** is responsible for administering the estate.

Those are different roles.

If there is no will and probate administration is required, an administrator may need to be appointed. If there are several heirs, that does not necessarily mean all of them need to run the estate together.

The important thing for you is not memorizing the terminology.

It is finding out:

**Who is entitled to the property, and who is currently authorized to act for the estate?**

# Do I Inherit the Debts?

This is where many people understandably get nervous.

**Generally, being a relative or inheriting property does not by itself make the deceased person’s debts your personal debts.**

Valid debts are generally handled through the estate. There can be exceptions—for example, if you were already legally responsible for a particular debt—but simply becoming an heir does not normally turn a deceased relative’s credit-card balance into your personal bill.

The more important distinction is this:

**A debt can reduce what you inherit without becoming a debt you personally owe.**

Suppose the house is worth $200,000.

That does **not** mean the heirs have inherited $200,000.

There could still be:

* A mortgage.
* Property-tax obligations.
* Liens against the property.
* Estate expenses.
* Other valid debts.

Those obligations can affect what remains before assets are distributed.

So the next question becomes much more practical.

# Could Nothing Be Left for the Heirs?

**Yes. It is possible for an estate to have assets but leave little or nothing for the heirs after valid obligations are handled.**

That sounds alarming, but you don’t need a complete accounting on day one.

You need a working picture.

Think about it this way:

**What could the house produce?**
minus
**What needs to be paid?**
equals
**What might remain.**

For the house itself, a useful starting point is:

**Potential sale proceeds − mortgage/liens − selling costs = estimated proceeds from the property**

That still isn’t necessarily your inheritance. The wider estate may have other assets, debts, expenses, or tax issues.

Ohio law establishes priorities for paying estate obligations when available assets are insufficient, so an estate cannot simply distribute its money to the heirs first and leave valid claims unpaid. ([Ohio Revised Code][2])

But there is an important practical complication:

**You may not know every number yet.**

Banks and other institutions may require evidence that someone has authority to act for the estate before providing certain account information. So sometimes you cannot perfectly answer “Is this estate worth it?” before beginning the process that gives someone authority to obtain the missing information.

That uncertainty is normal.

Start with what you can establish, and update the picture as the missing numbers become available.

# When Can I Sell the House?

This is probably the question you ultimately care about most.

**You do not necessarily have to wait until the entire probate process is finished before a house can be sold.**

What matters is whether the person attempting to sell has the required authority and whether the applicable requirements for that property have been satisfied.

Ohio provides different routes depending on the circumstances. For example, a qualified executor or administrator who has an appropriate power of sale under a will can sell without obtaining a separate probate-court order authorizing that sale. Ohio law also provides other procedures involving consent or court proceedings in other circumstances. ([Ohio Revised Code ?2113.39][3]; [?2127.011][6]; [?2127.04][7])

You do not need to master those procedures yourself to understand the important point:

**“Probate is still open” does not automatically mean “the house cannot be sold.”**

The better question is:

> **What still has to happen before someone has authority to complete the sale of this house?**

Once that answer is clear, you have a much better idea of how close you actually are to being able to sell.

# What Should I Figure Out Next?

At this point, you do not need twenty more questions.

You need clarity in three areas.

## 1. The legal picture

You need to know how the house passes after death, who the heirs or beneficiaries are, and who has—or needs to obtain—authority to act for the estate.

That tells you **whether and when the house can move forward**.

## 2. The debt picture

Gather what you can about the mortgage, property taxes, liens, and other potential estate obligations.

You may not be able to identify everything immediately. That is okay.

The goal is to move from:

> “I have no idea what she owed.”

toward:

> “I know what we’ve confirmed, what remains unknown, and what information we still need.”

## 3. The property-value picture

You need some idea of what the house is actually worth in its current condition.

# Deciding What to Do With the House

You started with:

> “Someone died and left me a house. I have no idea what to do.”

Now the problem is smaller.

You’re trying to establish:

**Who can act?**
**What does the estate owe?**
**What is the house worth?**

Once those answers become clearer, you can make the next decision with much better information.

Maybe you keep the house.

Maybe you rent it.

Maybe you sell it.

And if you decide to sell, you can then compare whether it makes sense to prepare and list the property, sell it as-is, or consider a direct offer.

You do not need to solve all of those decisions at once.

First, understand where you stand.

Then decide what to do with the house.
