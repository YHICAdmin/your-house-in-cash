# Article

If you are going through a divorce, the house can feel like one more overwhelming decision in an already difficult situation.

Maybe both of you are still living there.

Maybe one person has moved out.

Maybe neither of you can afford the mortgage alone.

Maybe you agree that selling is the right answer but cannot agree on the price, repairs, or timing.

You do not need to solve every part of the divorce before you begin to understand the house.

Start with a simpler question:

**What needs to happen with this property so that both people can move forward?**

## First, Understand That the House Is Part of a Bigger Financial Picture

The house is important, but it is usually not the only asset or debt involved in a divorce.

Ohio courts determine what is marital property and what is separate property, then divide property equitably. Property acquired during the marriage is generally marital property, but the facts matter, and title alone does not determine whether an asset is marital or separate property. [Ohio Revised Code §3105.171](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-3105.171)

That means you should not assume either of these:

> “The house is only in my spouse’s name, so I have no interest in it.”

or:

> “We both own the house, so we automatically split everything exactly in half.”

The legal outcome depends on the circumstances, the agreements involved, and, if necessary, the court’s orders.

For now, the practical goal is to understand what you are dealing with.

## Start With the Basic Facts

Before deciding whether to sell, keep, or refinance the house, gather the information both sides will need.

That includes:

* Who is on the deed
* Who is on the mortgage
* The current mortgage payoff amount
* Estimated market value
* Property taxes, insurance, and HOA costs, if applicable
* Any liens or home-equity loans
* The monthly cost of keeping the house
* Any repairs the house needs
* Whether either person can realistically afford to keep it

You are trying to answer two questions:

**What is the house worth?**

and

**What is it costing us to keep it?**

Those answers make the rest of the conversation much clearer.

## Estimate the Equity Before Debating the Sale Price

A house worth $250,000 does not automatically create $250,000 to divide.

The useful starting point is:

**Estimated sale price**

minus

**Mortgage payoff and other property-related obligations**

minus

**Selling costs**

equals

**Estimated equity from the sale**

For example, if the house could sell for $250,000 and the mortgage payoff is $170,000, there may be meaningful equity.

But the final amount can still be affected by commissions, title and closing costs, repairs, concessions, liens, or other obligations.

You do not need a perfect number at the beginning.

You need a realistic enough picture to understand whether keeping the house, buying out the other spouse, or selling it is financially possible.

## There Are Usually Three Main Paths

### 1. One Person Keeps the House

Sometimes one spouse wants to remain in the home.

That can be especially important when children are involved, when the house is affordable for one person to maintain, or when moving immediately would create an unnecessary disruption.

If one person keeps the house, the important questions are:

* Can that person actually afford the mortgage, taxes, insurance, repairs, and upkeep?
* Is refinancing necessary to remove the other person from the mortgage?
* How will the other person’s share of the equity be addressed?
* What does the divorce agreement or court order require?

Wanting to keep the house and being able to keep the house are not always the same thing.

A buyout can work well when the finances support it.

But it should be based on real numbers, not only on the emotional importance of the home.

### 2. Keep the House Jointly for a Period of Time

Some couples decide not to sell immediately.

For example, they may agree that one person stays in the house for a defined period, or that the property will be sold later.

This can create short-term stability, but it also means both people may remain connected to the property financially.

Before choosing this path, make sure the arrangement is specific.

Who pays the mortgage?

Who pays for repairs?

Who pays the taxes and insurance?

What happens if a payment is missed?

When exactly will the house be sold or refinanced?

A vague agreement to “deal with the house later” can become more complicated over time.

### 3. Sell the House and Divide the Proceeds

Selling is often the clearest option when neither person can comfortably afford the house alone, when both people need their equity to move forward, or when keeping the property would create ongoing conflict.

A sale turns the property into a known number.

That does not mean selling is always easy.

You may still need to agree on:

* Whether to make repairs before selling
* How the house will be cleaned or prepared
* How showings will work
* Whether either person will remain in the house during the sale
* The list price or the type of offer to accept
* How costs will be handled before closing
* How the proceeds will be distributed

But once the property is sold, there is no longer a shared house, shared mortgage, or future repair decision holding both people together.

## If You Agree to Sell, Decide How You Will Sell

There are different ways to sell a house during a divorce.

A traditional listing may make sense when you both have time, the home is reasonably marketable, and maximizing exposure is the priority.

That process may include cleaning, repairs, photographs, showings, inspections, buyer financing, and negotiations.

A direct as-is sale may make more sense when the house needs work, neither person wants to manage preparation, or the priority is a more straightforward closing timeline.

Neither approach is automatically better.

The question is:

**Which process gives both of you the outcome you need with the least additional conflict and uncertainty?**

Do not compare only a potential list price with a direct offer.

Compare the estimated net proceeds after repairs, commissions, carrying costs, time, and the work required to get there.

## What If You Cannot Agree?

This is where the house can become especially difficult.

One person may want to sell immediately. The other may want to stay.

One person may believe the house is worth much more than the other does.

One person may want to make repairs. The other may not want to spend more money.

You do not need to resolve that conflict by making unilateral decisions about the property.

If you do not have an agreement or clear authority to move forward, speak with your divorce attorney or another qualified Ohio legal professional before signing a listing agreement, accepting an offer, refinancing, or making a major decision involving the home.

Ohio Legal Help notes that a divorce can take months or longer, and temporary orders may address property division or debt payments while the case is pending. [Ohio Legal Help](https://www.ohiolegalhelp.org/topic/divorce)

## Start With Clarity, Not Pressure

Divorce does not automatically mean you have to sell the house.

One person may keep it. You may hold it temporarily. Or selling may be the most practical way for both people to move forward.

The key is to avoid treating the house as a separate problem from the rest of the financial and legal process.

First, understand the ownership, mortgage, equity, and monthly cost.

Then determine what each person can realistically afford and what authority or agreement is needed.

Once those pieces are clear, you can decide whether keeping, refinancing, or selling the house makes the most sense.

## Questions and Answers

### Can We Sell the House Before the Divorce Is Final?

Sometimes, but the answer depends on your agreement, the stage of the case, and any court orders. Before signing a listing agreement, accepting an offer, refinancing, or making another major decision about the house, make sure both spouses have agreed or that you have the legal authority to move forward. A divorce attorney or qualified Ohio legal professional can explain what applies to your situation.

### What If Only One Spouse Is on the Deed or Mortgage?

The name on the deed or mortgage does not by itself settle every question about the house in a divorce. Ohio courts determine whether property is marital or separate based on the facts and divide property equitably. The mortgage also remains a separate financial obligation until it is paid off, refinanced, or otherwise addressed. Get advice from your attorney before assuming that title or loan paperwork alone decides the outcome.

### What Happens If One Person Moves Out but Is Still on the Mortgage?

Moving out does not automatically remove someone from the mortgage. If both people signed the loan, missed payments can still affect both borrowers until the loan is paid off, refinanced, or otherwise resolved. Decide clearly who is responsible for payments, taxes, insurance, repairs, and upkeep while the house is still jointly connected to both of you.

### Do We Need to Repair the House Before Selling?

Not necessarily. A traditional listing may involve repairs, cleaning, photographs, showings, inspections, and buyer negotiations. A direct as-is sale can be an option if both people agree to sell and do not want to take on preparation or repair work first. Compare the likely net proceeds after repair costs, commissions, carrying costs, and time—not only the possible sale price.

### What If We Cannot Agree on What to Do With the House?

Do not make unilateral decisions about a shared property if you do not have an agreement or clear authority to act. A disagreement about selling, repairs, price, or timing may need to be addressed through your attorneys, mediation, or the court process. If there is coercion, domestic violence, or any safety concern, prioritize safety and appropriate legal support before focusing on the property.
