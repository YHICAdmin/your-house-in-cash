# How to Find and Vet Cash Home Buyers in Ohio

Finding a cash home buyer is usually easy. Knowing whether that buyer can actually get the job done is the part that deserves attention. Before you sign, a serious buyer should be able to explain the price, provide evidence of funds, make a meaningful earnest-money commitment, and show how the closing will be handled.

The goal is not to find the first cash home buyer who responds. It is to build a short list, ask each candidate the same practical questions, and compare the terms that show how reliably—and how quickly—the sale can close.

[Your House in Cash](/get-cash-offer) is one direct, as-is option you can include in that comparison. A no-obligation cash offer gives you another set of price and closing terms to review alongside other candidates.

## Where to find cash-buyer candidates

Cash buyers can be especially relevant when a house needs repairs, updates, cleaning, or other work that makes an as-is sale more practical. Those properties often attract a more specific type of buyer—one prepared to take on the work after closing. You may find those buyers through an online search, a referral, an advertisement, or a post in your community. Use more than one source so you are not deciding based on the first person who contacts you. The goal at this stage is simply to create a short list of candidates you can evaluate using the same questions and standards.

- **Google:** Search terms such as “cash home buyers in Ohio” or “sell my house as-is in [your city].” Look beyond the ads and review the company website, contact information, reviews, and whether the business clearly identifies who it is.
- **Personal referrals:** A referral can be useful when the person referring you can explain what actually happened during the transaction—not simply that they saw an advertisement.
- **Social media:** Search local Facebook groups, neighborhood pages, and other community discussions for cash-home-buyer recommendations or experiences. Treat a post or comment as a lead, then verify the buyer independently.
- **Direct mail and local advertising:** Letters, postcards, signs, and online ads can introduce you to local buyers. Treat them as a starting point for your list, then apply the same vetting questions to every candidate.

## How to vet a cash home buyer

A buyer’s promise to pay cash is not the same as proof that the transaction is ready to close. This is where proof of funds, earnest money, and the title company fit together.

### Ask for proof of funds

Ask the buyer to provide documentation showing that it has access to the funds needed for the purchase. The name on that documentation should make sense alongside the name on the offer. If an LLC is buying the house but the documentation belongs to a different person or business, ask for a clear explanation of the connection.

Proof of funds does not decide whether an offer is right for you. It helps you evaluate whether the buyer appears able to perform under the agreement.

### Review the earnest-money terms

Earnest money is a deposit made under a signed purchase agreement. It can demonstrate that the buyer has committed to the transaction, but its value depends on the contract terms: the amount, the deadline for depositing it, who will hold it, and when the buyer can recover it.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau describes earnest money as a good-faith deposit that may be held by a seller or a third party, including a title company. If the contract permits termination, the deposit may be returned; if a buyer fails to perform in good faith, it may be forfeited.[1]

Ask these questions before you sign:

- How much earnest money will be deposited?
- When will it be deposited?
- Who will hold it?
- What contract conditions allow the buyer to cancel?
- What happens to the deposit if the buyer does not close?

### Confirm the title company and closing plan

Ask which title company will handle the closing, then contact that company directly using contact details you independently find. Confirm that it has the correct buyer name, property address, purchase contract, and earnest-money instructions.

You can also ask whether the title company has worked with the buyer before and whether it can speak to the buyer's track record of completing transactions.

## Watch for red flags before you sign

Not every low offer is a red flag, and not every fast offer is a problem. The concern is whether the buyer is clear, consistent, and willing to put the important terms in writing.

Be cautious when a buyer:

- Pressures you to sign immediately or discourages you from reading the agreement.
- Will not provide proof of funds or explain who is buying.
- Uses a contract with broad cancellation rights but little meaningful commitment.
- Cannot identify the title company or gives instructions that do not match the title company’s information.
- Changes the price after agreement without a clear, contract-based reason.
- Promises a quick closing but cannot explain the actual timeline.
- Avoids questions about earnest money, inspection periods, or assignment rights.

A buyer should be able to explain its price, its timeline, and the terms that could change the deal.

## Compare the complete offers

A higher number is not automatically the stronger offer. Compare each candidate’s price, proof of funds, earnest money, cancellation terms, assignment rights, title company, expected closing date, and any requests for repairs, credits, or access to the property.

If your house needs substantial work, compare the offer with the cost and time of making those repairs before deciding which sale path makes sense. Our guide to [selling a house with major repairs](/major-repairs) explains that comparison in more detail.

The condition and use of the house may affect those terms. If it is vacant, for example, holding costs and timing may matter as much as the price; our [vacant-property guide](/vacant-property) explains why vacant houses often need a clear plan. If it is a rental with tenants, access and possession can change the sale process; our [landlord guide](/landlords) covers those considerations.

The goal is not to find a perfect buyer. It is to understand exactly what each buyer is offering, how likely the transaction is to close, and what you would receive if it does.
