# What to Do When Tenants Stop Paying Rent in Ohio

When rent is late, the most important thing is to slow down enough to follow the legal process in the right order. In Ohio, an eviction for nonpayment is a court process—not a lock change, utility shutoff, or a landlord deciding on their own that the tenancy is over.

This guide is general information for Ohio rental-property owners, not legal advice. Your lease, the payment history, local court rules, and any defenses or assistance program involved can change the result. An eviction attorney can help you understand how the process applies to your rental.

## Start with the lease and a conversation

Before moving forward, try to reach the tenant and understand why the rent is late. A short conversation may tell you whether this is a temporary problem, a misunderstanding, or something that needs a more formal response.

If you decide to give the tenant a few days to pay, be clear about the amount due and the date you expect payment. Keep a simple written record of the amount due, the dates rent was due, any payment received, and every conversation or message about payment.

Do not wait too long without a plan. Extra time can push back the entire eviction timeline if rent still is not paid. Check the lease for any grace-period or late-fee terms, and get legal guidance before moving forward if the situation is unclear or disputed.

## Be cautious about accepting partial rent

If you have decided to pursue an eviction based on nonpayment, do not casually accept a partial-rent payment without first understanding how it may affect the notice and court case. A partial payment can create arguments about the balance due, the landlord's intent, or whether a notice has been waived. The outcome can depend on the lease wording, payment timing, and local practice.

That does not mean every partial payment has the same legal result. It means this is a point where a landlord should make a deliberate decision, document it, and have an Ohio eviction attorney review the situation before accepting funds or agreeing to a payment arrangement. Do not make promises you do not intend to keep, and do not use a payment arrangement as a substitute for clear written documentation.

## Serve the required three-day notice

If the tenant still has not paid after the time you agreed on—and you decide not to accept a partial payment—the next step is a written three-day notice to leave. Ohio law specifies the required warning language and allows service by certified mail, personal delivery, or leaving a written copy at the tenant's usual residence or the rental property. ([Ohio Revised Code § 1923.04][1])

Wait three days. If the tenant still has not moved out or paid, you can file for eviction in court. The notice does not remove the tenant by itself. Keep a copy of the notice and record how and when it was served, since mistakes can slow the case down.

Don't try to force the tenant out yourself. Ohio law prohibits a landlord from excluding a tenant, turning off utilities or services, or using threats to recover possession outside the legal process. ([Ohio Revised Code § 5321.15][2])

## Involve an eviction attorney early

Because an eviction can affect possession, rent claims, the tenant's defenses, and the condition of the property, getting an Ohio eviction attorney involved early can be worthwhile. An attorney can review the lease, payment ledger, notices, and local court requirements before a filing is made.

This is particularly important if the tenant has made a partial payment, says repairs are unresolved, has deposited rent with the court, receives housing assistance, is in military service, or has raised discrimination or retaliation concerns. Those facts do not necessarily stop an eviction, but they can change what needs to happen next.

Bring the lease, ledger, copies of notices, communication records, and any relevant inspection or repair records. A complete file makes it easier to assess the case before deadlines or procedural mistakes add time and cost.

## File and attend eviction court

If the tenant does not leave after the notice period, the landlord can file an eviction action in the court with jurisdiction over the rental property. The court issues the summons and complaint. For a possession claim, Ohio law requires service at least seven days before the scheduled trial date, and the hearing cannot be scheduled sooner than seven days after service is complete. ([Ohio Revised Code § 1923.06][3])

At the hearing, be prepared to show the lease, the rent ledger, the three-day notice, proof of service, and the facts supporting the claim. You can also ask the court for unpaid rent, but that part of the case may take longer.

Court procedures, filing fees, required forms, and hearing schedules vary by county and municipal court. Follow the specific court's instructions and the advice of the attorney handling the matter.

## Implement the eviction only after a court order

Winning a possession judgment does not authorize a landlord to remove the tenant personally. After a judgment for restitution, the court can issue a writ of execution at the request of the landlord, the landlord's agent, or attorney. The writ directs an authorized sheriff, police officer, constable, or bailiff to restore possession to the landlord. ([Ohio Revised Code § 1923.13][4])

For most cases, the officer must execute the writ within ten days after receiving it, subject to any appeal or stay ordered by the court. ([Ohio Revised Code § 1923.14][5]) Ask the court clerk or your attorney about the local scheduling and property-removal process. Do not dispose of belongings or change locks until the authorized process permits it.
