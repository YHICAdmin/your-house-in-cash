# Section 8 for Landlords in Cuyahoga County: A Practical Guide

Accepting a housing voucher can help Cuyahoga County landlords reach more prospective tenants and receive part of the rent from the housing authority. But a voucher is not automatically the right fit for every rental. The decision comes down to three things: whether the rent works for the property, whether the unit meets inspection requirements, and whether you are comfortable with CMHA's process.

## What is Section 8?

Section 8 usually means the federal Housing Choice Voucher program. The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development funds the program, while local public housing agencies administer it. In Cuyahoga County, the Cuyahoga Metropolitan Housing Authority, commonly called CMHA, administers the program for many local properties.[1]

A voucher household finds a rental unit, the housing authority reviews the proposed rental arrangement, and the housing authority pays an approved share of the rent to the landlord. The tenant remains responsible for the balance assigned to them and for following the lease.

It is not a separate Section 8 program in every city. The federal framework is shared, but payment standards, inspections, local paperwork, and availability are administered locally.

## Why landlords may accept voucher tenants

The appeal for many owners is straightforward: after the rental is approved, CMHA pays its share of the housing-assistance payment directly to the landlord. That can make cash flow more predictable for that part of the rent.

Other potential benefits include:

- Access to a wider group of applicants.
- Strong demand for two- and three-bedroom homes in many local neighborhoods.
- A defined approval process for the unit and proposed rent.
- An opportunity to compare the voucher market with the rent an owner expects to receive from a conventional tenant.

Voucher participation does not replace ordinary property management. The landlord still chooses whether an applicant meets lawful, consistently applied screening standards, maintains the home, and handles lease enforcement.

## What can go wrong with a Section 8 rental?

The process can be a good fit for one property and a poor fit for another. The main challenges are usually operational, rather than the fact that a tenant uses a voucher.

- **The unit may not pass inspection right away:** The home must meet the program's housing-quality requirements. Repairs can delay move-in if the unit is not ready.
- **Your full asking rent may not be approved:** The agency reviews whether the proposed rent is reasonable for comparable units. An owner may not receive approval for the full asking rent.
- **Paperwork can delay the start date:** The approval request, inspection, lease, and housing-assistance-payment contract must be completed before the new arrangement is fully in place.
- **You still have to collect the tenant's share:** The housing authority's payment covers only its approved portion. The tenant is still responsible for their share of the rent and for lease obligations.
- **Utilities can change the math:** Tenant-paid utilities affect the gross-rent calculation. A rent that looks workable on its own may not work after the applicable utility allowance is included.
- **An eviction means extra notices and coordination:** A voucher does not create a separate eviction court process. However, a landlord must follow the usual Ohio court process, give written grounds for ending the lease during its term, and send CMHA a copy of any eviction notice given to the tenant. Those added program requirements can make the process more administrative.[8]

These are reasons to understand the process before advertising, not reasons to assume anything about an individual voucher holder. Use the same lawful, written screening standards for every applicant.

## CMHA payment standards for two- and three-bedroom rentals

CMHA's payment standard is the official benchmark used to calculate voucher assistance. It is based on the property's ZIP code and the voucher bedroom size. It is not a guaranteed rent payment or an automatic approval of an owner's asking rent.[2]

| City / main ZIP | Two-bedroom payment standard | Three-bedroom payment standard |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Euclid / 44123 | $1,446 | $1,864 |
| Maple Heights / 44137 | $1,819 | $2,339 |
| Garfield Heights / 44125 | $1,529 | $1,969 |
| Parma / 44134 | $1,551 | $1,991 |
| Cleveland Heights / 44118 | $1,683 | $2,169 |

These FY 2026 figures are effective January 1, 2026. They apply to the ZIP shown, not every address in the city. Always check the property's exact ZIP in CMHA's Rent Determination tool before setting a rent.[2]

## What this means for a landlord

The most useful way to evaluate a voucher rental is property by property. Start with the actual address, bedroom count, asking rent, and tenant-paid utilities. Then compare the result with current local listings and CMHA's ZIP-specific payment standard. That shows whether the unit is positioned for the voucher market before the paperwork begins.

For an owner deciding whether to keep operating a rental or sell instead, rental income is only one side of the decision. Vacancy, repairs, inspections, turnover, and the time required to manage the property all matter too.
