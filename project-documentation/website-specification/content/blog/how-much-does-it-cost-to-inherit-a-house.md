# How Much Does It Cost to Inherit a House?

Inheriting a house does not always mean you need to spend thousands of dollars immediately. But it can create expenses before the estate has money available, especially if the house is vacant or probate is needed.

The helpful question is not just, “How much does probate cost?” It is:

**What may need to be paid before the house or estate can move forward—and could I lose that money?**

The answer depends on the house, the estate, and whether probate is necessary. If you are still figuring out who has authority over the house and what your options are, start with our [guide to inheriting a house](/inherited-property).

## House insurance and maintenance costs

The house does not pause when its owner dies. Mortgage payments, property taxes, insurance, utilities, and basic maintenance can still be due.

If the house is vacant, the immediate goal is usually to prevent avoidable damage or loss—not to renovate it. That can mean keeping it secure, addressing a leak, maintaining the heat when needed, and confirming that its insurance remains in place.

The actual cost varies widely. A house in good condition may need only its normal bills and basic upkeep. A house with an overdue mortgage, a water problem, delinquent taxes, or needed repairs can become much more expensive.

It is useful to separate these costs into two groups:

- **Costs already tied to the house:** mortgage payments, property taxes, insurance, utilities, and recorded liens.
- **New costs of holding the house:** securing it, mowing or snow removal, emergency repairs, or ongoing maintenance while the estate is being handled.

These are generally estate or property expenses, not automatically personal debts of the heir. But somebody may need to advance money before the estate has access to funds. Keep a clear record of every payment and what it was for.

## Probate costs

Probate is the court process used to identify estate assets, pay valid debts and expenses, and distribute what remains. It also gives an executor or administrator legal authority to handle property that is part of the estate.

Not every inherited house needs probate. A trust, survivorship ownership, or a valid transfer-on-death designation can change how a house passes after death. Probate itself may involve a few hundred dollars in court filing fees. The larger cost, when there is one, is often legal help and the work involved in handling a house, debts, and estate paperwork.

### Legal fees

Legal fees are often the largest probate-related expense. Many estates use an attorney because the paperwork and responsibility can become more involved when there is a house, multiple heirs, debts, or a sale. There is no single standard total: an attorney may charge a flat fee, hourly fee, or another reasonable arrangement based on the work involved.

For a house sold through probate in Cuyahoga County, the court's attorney-fee guideline is $4,500 on $100,000 in sale proceeds, $8,000 on $200,000, and $11,500 on $300,000. This is a guideline, not a fixed price. ([Cuyahoga County Probate Court][1])

## Could you lose money?

**Yes. There is a real risk when someone pays expenses out of pocket before the estate has money available.**

For example, a family member may pay the court filing fee, keep the insurance active, cover an emergency repair, or pay for help with probate paperwork. If the estate has enough assets, those administration expenses are generally paid before most debts and before heirs receive a distribution. ([Ohio Revised Code][2])

But an estate may not have enough money to repay every expense. That can happen when the house is worth less than the mortgage and liens, when taxes and repairs have built up, or when the deceased person left more valid debts than assets.

That does not usually make an heir personally responsible for the deceased person's debts. It does mean the house and other estate assets may leave little—or nothing—after the estate's obligations are handled.

Before putting your own money into the house, try to get a working picture of three things:

1. **What does the estate own?** This includes the house, accounts, vehicles, and other assets.
2. **What does the estate owe?** Start with the mortgage, property taxes, known liens, utilities, and other bills.
3. **What will it cost to keep the house from getting worse?** Focus first on insurance, security, urgent repairs, and the basic bills needed to protect the property.

You may not have every answer at the start. The point is to understand the risk before paying for optional work, repairs, or improvements that may not be recovered.

## The number that matters: what could be left?

The most useful calculation is not the house's estimated value by itself. It is what may remain after the estate's obligations are handled:

**Estimated house value**  
minus **mortgage, liens, taxes, estate expenses, and selling costs**  
equals **an estimate of what may remain**

That estimate may change as more information becomes available. But it is a much better starting point than assuming the house's value is the amount the family will inherit.
