# Mortgage Forbearance What Homeowners Need to Know

Mortgage forbearance can give you temporary room to breathe when a short-term hardship makes your regular payment impossible. It is not forgiveness: the unpaid amount still has to be addressed later.

That distinction matters. Forbearance can be a useful bridge when you have a realistic reason to expect your finances to recover. If the mortgage will still be unaffordable after the hardship ends, a pause alone may only postpone a harder decision.

## What is mortgage forbearance?

Forbearance is an agreement in which a mortgage servicer temporarily allows smaller payments or a pause in payments. The arrangement is for a defined period and under terms set by the servicer or lender. You still owe the payments that were reduced or skipped. ([Consumer Financial Protection Bureau][1])

Forbearance is one type of mortgage assistance, sometimes called loss mitigation. It does not transfer ownership of the house, erase your mortgage balance, or automatically stop every part of a foreclosure situation. The exact effect depends on your loan, the agreement, and where you are in the process.

If you are already behind or have received foreclosure papers, see our [foreclosure situation guide](/foreclosure) for a clearer view of the stages and the decisions that may matter most.

## When forbearance might be a solution

Forbearance is usually designed for a temporary problem, not a permanent affordability problem. It may fit when a specific setback has interrupted your income or created unusual expenses, but you expect to resume your normal payment after a limited period.

Examples can include a temporary job loss with a new job beginning soon, a medical event, or damage from a disaster. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau lists job loss, unexpected medical costs, and natural-disaster damage as examples of hardships for which forbearance can help. ([Consumer Financial Protection Bureau][1])

The practical question is not only whether you qualify. It is whether the plan leaves you with a payment you can realistically make when it ends. If you expect to resume the normal payment but cannot afford an added catch-up amount, the repayment method is just as important as the temporary relief.

## What the process usually looks like

The process begins with your mortgage servicer, the company shown on your monthly statement that collects and manages your mortgage payment. The servicer reviews the hardship and the loan's available options; those can vary by loan type and investor. ([Consumer Financial Protection Bureau][2])

1. **Explain what changed.** The servicer may ask why you cannot make the payment, whether the hardship is temporary or ongoing, and for information about income, expenses, and available assets.
2. **Review the proposed payment relief.** Confirm whether payments will be paused or reduced, the amount due during the plan, and how long the plan lasts.
3. **Understand the repayment path before agreeing.** Ask how the unpaid balance will be handled once the forbearance period ends and whether interest continues to accrue under the agreement.
4. **Keep the terms and notices.** The plan should be clear enough to show the payment amount, due dates, end date, and what happens next.
5. **Plan for the end of forbearance.** Before the plan expires, understand which exit option applies to you and whether the resulting payment fits your budget.

The CFPB says that repayment can take several forms, including a lump-sum payment, a temporary repayment plan, a deferral to the end of the loan, or a loan modification. What is available depends on the loan and the servicer's rules. ([Consumer Financial Protection Bureau][3])

## Where things can get complicated

The word “forbearance” describes the temporary relief, not necessarily the full solution. The complications usually appear in the details of the agreement and at the end of the plan.

### The repayment method can change the real cost

Some plans require missed amounts to be paid back over a short period, which can raise the monthly payment. Others may move missed payments to the end of the mortgage or put them into a separate amount due when the home is sold, refinanced, or the mortgage ends. A lower payment now can still mean a larger obligation later. ([Consumer Financial Protection Bureau][3])

### Interest and escrow may still matter

Forbearance does not necessarily stop interest from accruing on unpaid amounts. Your mortgage payment can also include taxes and insurance, so ask how each part of the payment is treated under the specific agreement. The answer is loan- and plan-specific. ([Consumer Financial Protection Bureau][1])

### A forbearance agreement is not the same as a completed long-term plan

You may need a separate repayment plan, deferral, or loan modification after forbearance ends. A repayment plan adds part of the past-due amount to the regular monthly payment, so it only works if that higher payment is manageable. ([Consumer Financial Protection Bureau][4])

### Foreclosure timing does not disappear from the picture

If foreclosure has already begun, documents and deadlines still matter. Do not assume that a request for help changes every deadline or that an incomplete request produces the same protections as an approved plan. Knowing your stage is important; our [foreclosure situation guide](/foreclosure) explains the difference between missed payments, notices, a lawsuit, and a Sheriff's Sale.

## The decision to focus on

Forbearance can be valuable when it helps you get through a short-term disruption and return to a mortgage you can afford. It is less likely to solve the problem when the regular mortgage payment remains out of reach after the disruption ends.

Before agreeing, make sure you can answer three questions: What will I owe during the plan? What happens to the unpaid amount? What will my payment be when the plan ends?
