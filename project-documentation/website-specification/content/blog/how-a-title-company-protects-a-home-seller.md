# How a Title Company Helps Protect a Home Seller

Selling a house involves more than agreeing on a price and signing a deed.

Before ownership can change hands, the closing has to account for the property’s recorded history, any mortgages or claims attached to it, and the money that must be paid from the sale. That is where a title company often becomes part of the process.

A title company does not guarantee that every sale will be simple, and it does not replace legal advice when there is a dispute. But its work can uncover title problems early, organize the closing, and document how money and ownership move from seller to buyer.

## What Is a Title Company?

In a typical Ohio home sale, a title company researches the property’s title history, prepares or coordinates closing documents, and may act as the settlement agent that handles the closing.

“Title” is not a single paper. It is the legal ownership interest in a property and the record of rights or claims connected to it. Ohio law defines title insurance as protection against loss or damage from liens, encumbrances, defects in title, or unmarketable title. [Ohio Revised Code Chapter 3953](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/chapter-3953) sets out that definition.

The company handling a closing is often called a title company, settlement agent, or closing agent. In much of the country, the closing agent works through a title insurance company; the exact arrangement can vary by state and transaction. [The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/close/shop-for-title-insurance-and-other-closing-services/) that title services can include the title search, title insurance, and the fee for the agent conducting the closing.

## How a Title Search Helps a Seller

One of the first jobs is to examine public records connected to the property. The search can reveal items that may need to be addressed before closing, such as:

* An existing mortgage or home-equity loan
* Unpaid property taxes
* A judgment lien or mechanic’s lien
* A prior deed, ownership, or recording issue
* Easements or other recorded restrictions
* Estate or probate questions when an owner has died

Finding one of these items does not automatically stop a sale. It tells the parties what must be understood, paid, released, corrected, or otherwise resolved for the transaction to move forward.

For a seller, that is useful because an old issue can be identified before the final days of the closing. A paid-off loan, for example, may still appear in the records if its release was not recorded correctly. A title search gives the sale a chance to address that issue rather than discovering it after a buyer expects to close.

## How the Closing Process Can Protect the Seller

The title company’s protection for a seller is largely procedural. It helps put the transaction into an organized, documented process rather than leaving the deed, payoff funds, and closing paperwork to be handled informally.

At closing, the settlement agent can coordinate the documents, receive funds, calculate disbursements, pay approved items from sale proceeds, and arrange recording of the deed and other documents. The closing statement shows how the money is allocated.

That process matters when the seller still has a mortgage. Instead of the seller trying to separately coordinate a payoff after the house has transferred, the closing can use sale proceeds to satisfy the approved payoff as part of the transaction. If liens, taxes, or other approved charges must be paid, they may also appear as line items that affect the seller’s net proceeds.

The seller should still read every document and ask questions about anything unexpected. The CFPB advises consumers to review closing documents carefully and not sign until the documents match their expectations. [Its closing guidance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/close/close-the-deal/) also explains that a settlement agent may be a title company, escrow officer, or attorney.

## Title Insurance Does Not Usually Insure the Seller

This distinction is important.

Title insurance is generally designed to protect the buyer, the buyer’s lender, or both against certain title defects that existed before the policy was issued. It is not automatically a policy that protects the seller from every future claim or expense.

A lender’s title policy protects the lender’s interest. An owner’s title policy protects the buyer’s ownership interest, subject to the policy’s terms, exclusions, and exceptions. [Ohio’s owner’s-title notice](https://codes.ohio.gov/assets/laws/administrative-code/pdfs/3901/0/7/3901-7-03_PH_FYR_C_APP1_20160819_1444.pdf) explains that a lender’s policy protects the lender, while an owner’s policy can protect the buyer from certain missed recorded claims.

For a seller, the practical benefit comes from identifying and addressing title issues before the deed transfers, using a documented closing process, and knowing what obligations are being paid from the proceeds. That is different from saying the title company takes over the seller’s legal responsibility for every problem connected to the property.

## What a Title Company Does Not Do

A title company is not automatically the seller’s attorney or personal advocate. It does not decide whether a seller must accept a buyer’s request, determine the value of the house, or make property-condition disclosures for the seller.

It also cannot make a lien or ownership dispute disappear simply because a sale is pending. Some issues are straightforward to clear; others may require more documentation, agreement from another party, or legal guidance. The right next step depends on the facts of the transaction.

If the property is inherited, owned by multiple people, connected to a divorce, subject to a judgment, or affected by an unresolved ownership dispute, the seller should be especially careful not to assume a standard closing will resolve every question.

## What Title and Closing Costs Can Include

There is no universal rule for which party pays every title or closing charge. The purchase agreement, local practice, lender requirements, and negotiations can all affect the answer.

Possible costs include:

* The title search or examination
* Title insurance premiums, if a policy is purchased
* Settlement or closing fees
* Recording fees
* Transfer taxes or conveyance charges, where applicable
* Mortgage payoff amounts, interest, and release-related charges
* Prorated property taxes, association charges, or utilities
* Seller concessions or credits that were agreed to in the contract

For a financed purchase, title and closing charges are generally shown on the Closing Disclosure. The CFPB notes that title-related fees can include the title search, lender’s title policy, and other services tied to issuing title insurance; the closing agent’s fee is also commonly part of title services. [See the CFPB’s explanation of title service fees](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-are-title-service-fees-en-157/).

The most useful number for a seller is usually not one individual fee. It is the expected **net proceeds**: the sale price minus agreed seller costs, mortgage payoff amounts, taxes or liens that must be paid, and other closing charges. A preliminary settlement statement can make that picture easier to understand before closing day.

## Questions to Ask Before Closing

You do not need to become an expert in title records to follow the transaction. You do need to understand what the closing documents say about your property and your money.

Useful questions include:

* Are there any title issues that could delay closing?
* What mortgage, lien, tax, or other payoff amounts will be taken from the sale proceeds?
* Which costs are assigned to me under the purchase agreement?
* What is my estimated net proceeds amount?
* What documents must I sign, and will any signatures need to be notarized?
* When will the deed be recorded and sale proceeds be disbursed?

Asking these questions early is not a sign that something is wrong. It is part of understanding how the sale is being completed.

A title company’s role is easy to overlook when a sale is moving smoothly. But its work can matter most when the property has an old loan, a lien, an inheritance question, or a recording issue that nobody noticed at first.
