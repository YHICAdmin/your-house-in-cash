# How to Know if You Could Qualify for a Short Sale

If you owe more on your mortgage than your house can sell for, a short sale may be one way to avoid a completed foreclosure. In a short sale, the mortgage servicer agrees to let the home be sold for less than the mortgage balance. It is not automatic, and it is not the same as simply putting the house up for sale: the servicer and, in some cases, the owner of the loan must approve it. ([Consumer Financial Protection Bureau][1])

## How to know if you could qualify for a short sale

There is no single rule that guarantees approval. Each mortgage has its own requirements, and the servicer reviews the full situation. But a short sale is more likely to be considered when the home's value is less than the amount owed, keeping the mortgage is no longer realistic, and the homeowner can document why they cannot continue making the payments.

The homeowner usually needs to complete a loss-mitigation application. Loss mitigation is the servicer's process for reviewing options that may help avoid foreclosure, such as a repayment plan, loan modification, forbearance, short sale, or deed in lieu of foreclosure. The application gives the servicer a picture of the homeowner's income, expenses, assets, and hardship. ([Consumer Financial Protection Bureau][2])

The homeowner must also find a buyer. The servicer reviews the proposed sale price and the terms of the offer before the sale can close. If there is more than one mortgage or lien on the house, approval may be needed from each lienholder. ([Consumer Financial Protection Bureau][3])

## Why a lender may approve a short sale

A lender may approve a short sale because foreclosure is often expensive, slow, and uncertain. If the lender forecloses, it may need to pay legal fees, keep the property insured, maintain it while vacant, cover taxes or association charges, and then sell it later. During that time, the house can lose value or need more repairs.

With a short sale, the homeowner finds a buyer and the lender reviews the offer before the sale closes. The lender may decide that receiving the sale proceeds now—even though they are less than the full mortgage balance—is a better outcome than spending more time and money taking the house through foreclosure. It is not automatic: the lender still needs to review the homeowner's financial situation, the home's value, and the proposed sale price.

## Documents a lender may ask you to provide

The exact list depends on the servicer and the loan, but the loss-mitigation application commonly asks for documents that show your financial situation and the reason you need help. Your servicer should tell you what it needs to complete its review. ([Consumer Financial Protection Bureau][4])

You may be asked to provide:

- A completed loss-mitigation or mortgage-assistance application.
- A hardship letter explaining what changed and why the mortgage payment is no longer manageable, along with supporting evidence when available.
- Recent pay stubs or other proof of income, such as benefit or award letters.
- Recent bank statements.
- Tax returns or other documents the servicer requests to verify income and assets.
- A recent mortgage statement and information about any other loans or liens against the house.
- The signed purchase offer and any sale documents the servicer needs to review the proposed short sale.

Send every page the servicer requests, even a page that appears blank, and keep copies of everything you submit. If the servicer says the application is incomplete, ask what is missing and return it by the stated deadline. A complete application gives the servicer what it needs to evaluate the available options. ([Consumer Financial Protection Bureau][5])
