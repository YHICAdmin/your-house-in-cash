# Cleveland Heights Point-of-Sale Inspection: A Real-World Case Study

If you are preparing to sell a house in Cleveland Heights, the point-of-sale inspection—usually called a POS—is a required part of the sale process. Cleveland Heights can also require an inspection outside of a sale—as in the real-world case discussed here, years after the property was purchased. When a sale is involved, the City requires owners to obtain a Certificate of Inspection before entering into an agreement and give the prospective buyer a copy before the contract is signed.[1]

The POS is not a buyer's private home inspection. It is the City's review of the property for compliance with local housing and building requirements. Its purpose is to identify the conditions the City requires to be addressed as part of the transfer process.

That distinction matters. A house can have a POS report with violations and still be sold, but the report, the buyer acknowledgement, and any required escrow need to be handled correctly before title can transfer.[2]

## What a Cleveland Heights POS includes

The City issues a Certificate of Inspection after the POS. It is valid for one year from the inspection date for a sale or other transfer of interest.[1]

The report is specific to the property. It can identify exterior or interior conditions that do not meet the City's requirements. Some items may be relatively limited; others can be classified as major, or Class A, violations.

Examples of Class A work listed by the City include:

- Major concrete repair or replacement, porch and step repair, deteriorated windows or doors, painting and related carpentry, roof or chimney work, and certain drainage items.[2]
- Foundation concerns, major HVAC, wall, ceiling, or floor breaches, and major plumbing or electrical repairs.[2]

The report itself determines what is cited at a particular house. It is not a general repair checklist and it is not a promise that the property has no other condition a buyer may discover.

## How the Cleveland Heights POS process works

**Step 1: Apply and schedule the inspection.** The owner or authorized agent applies through the City's process and schedules the inspection. The current fee is $200 for the first dwelling unit and $50 for each additional unit.[1]

**Step 2: Receive the Certificate of Inspection and violation list.** Cleveland Heights says inspection reports are normally issued within five working days. The certificate tells the seller and buyer what the City found.[3]

**Step 3: Give the buyer the certificate.** The seller provides the prospective buyer with the original Certificate of Inspection, plus a Certificate of Compliance if one is available, before the sale contract is executed. The buyer signs an acknowledgement showing receipt of the inspection certificate.[1]

**Step 4: Decide how violations will be handled.** The seller can complete the work before closing. A buyer can also assume violations. But when Class A violations remain, the City requires funds to be held in escrow before title transfer.[3]

**Step 5: Provide transfer information to the City.** Inspectional Services needs the buyer's signed acknowledgement, title-company information, and, if applicable, a letter confirming the escrowed amount. The City then sends the title company its release for transfer.[2]

**Step 6: Complete repairs and request reinspection.** Once cited items are completed, the owner or buyer can schedule a reinspection. If the work passes, the City can issue written authorization for escrow funds to be released.[3]

## A real-world City inspection case study: from notice to correction

I recently got a letter from the City about one of my rental properties in Cleveland Heights. We'd owned the house for years and had no plans to sell it, but the City notified us that it was time for another inspection.

The notice included a list of things we needed to fix. We took care of everything, and after the City came back to reinspect the property, they sent us a letter confirming that all the issues had been resolved.

You can [download the redacted case-study documents](/documents/cleveland-heights-inspection-case-study-redacted.pdf) to see the actual list of violations.

### What the notice looked like

The notice was broken down by area of the house: the garage exterior, yard, basement, stairs, kitchen, and bedrooms. Under each area, it listed the work that needed to be done and the City code section. By the time of the reinspection, only a couple of items were still open.

### The bigger items we had to fix

**Water-heater safety and venting.** We had to add a missing pilot cover to the water heater and correct the flue, which had a negative pitch.

**Smoke and carbon-monoxide detection.** The notice called for smoke detectors and carbon-monoxide detectors in several locations. The smoke-detector items were marked for immediate compliance, so we took care of those right away.

**Exterior maintenance and drainage.** We also had to address a deteriorating garage window/side-over, clean the gutters, and make the driveway drain operable. The notice covered both safety items inside the house and maintenance items outside.

### This inspection was manageable

Overall, this inspection was not too bad. The list had several items on it, but it could have been much more expensive if the inspector had cited heavier work.

For a sense of what larger violations can cost, our [Garfield Heights point-of-sale inspection guide](/blog/garfield-heights-point-of-sale-inspection) includes a list of violations and average repair prices. Garfield Heights is a different city and its POS process is not the same, but the cost of many repairs is similar.

## Why major repairs can change the decision

Some POS items are manageable repairs. Others can become a larger project. Roof, foundation, major electrical, plumbing, concrete, and significant exterior work are all among the types of conditions Cleveland Heights identifies as Class A violations.[2]

If the house needs several substantial repairs, the question is not automatically whether to fix everything before selling. It can help to compare the repair cost, escrow requirement, time involved, and likely sale outcome. Our guide to [what to consider when a house needs major repairs](/major-repairs) explains that comparison in more detail.

An as-is buyer may be willing to purchase a property with outstanding POS work. That does not remove the City's transfer requirements. Instead, it can change who completes the work and how the price, escrow, and closing paperwork are structured.

## Plan for the POS before the closing date

The Cleveland Heights POS is easier to manage when it begins early enough for everyone to understand the report, decide how outstanding work will be handled, and complete the City's transfer paperwork.
